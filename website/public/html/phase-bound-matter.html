Introduction

Position in the modular series

This paper is Paper II of four in the modular research series Emergent Spacetime Dynamics. The series is organised as a sequence of categorical layers, each composing on the previous via an explicit functorial lifting: Law I→LiftI→IILaw II→LiftII→IIILaw III→LiftIII→IVLaw IV.\text{Law I} \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathrm{Lift}_{\mathrm{I}\to\mathrm{II}}\,}\; \text{Law II} \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathrm{Lift}_{\mathrm{II}\to\mathrm{III}}\,}\; \text{Law III} \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathrm{Lift}_{\mathrm{III}\to\mathrm{IV}}\,}\; \text{Law IV.} Law I (Mathematical Formalisms, MagnetonIO Research) established the categorical grammar that all subsequent laws share: symmetric monoidal categories, dagger-compact closed categories for quantum systems, sheaves and topoi for local observables, operads and factorisation algebras for QFT-style composition, the matter–information functor M:𝑷𝒉𝒚𝒔→𝑰𝒏𝒇𝒐M:\mathbf{Phys}\to\mathbf{Info}, and a type-theoretic encoding of these structures. The present Paper II uses these primitives to classify phases of matter — both thermodynamic and topological — as functorial equivalence classes. We work entirely in the language of Law I.

We emphasise that the framework is modular, not unified. Each law contributes one self-standing mathematical layer; emergent properties at each layer arise from the composition of layers, not from any single overarching theory.

One-page recap of Law I

For convenience we collect the Law I primitives we will need, fixing notation and references for the remainder of the paper.

A symmetric monoidal category (𝒞,⊗,I,α,λ,ρ,σ)(\mathcal{C},\otimes,I,\alpha,\lambda,\rho,\sigma) consists of a category 𝒞\mathcal{C}, a tensor functor ⊗:𝒞×𝒞→𝒞{\otimes:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}}, a unit object II, and natural isomorphisms (associator α\alpha, unitors λ,ρ\lambda,\rho, symmetry σ\sigma) satisfying Mac Lane’s pentagon and triangle axioms together with the symmetry hexagons. Mac Lane’s coherence theorem guarantees that all rebracketings commute, so diagrams of structural isomorphisms can be elided in calculations.

A †\dagger-category is a category equipped with an involutive contravariant identity- on-objects functor †:𝒞op→𝒞\dagger:\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathcal{C}. A †\dagger-compact closed category additionally has duals A↦A*A\mapsto A^{*} with unit ηA:I→A*⊗A\eta_{A}:I\to A^{*}\otimes A and counit εA:A⊗A*→I\varepsilon_{A}:A\otimes A^{*}\to I. The category 𝑭𝑯𝒊𝒍𝒃\mathbf{FHilb} of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces with linear maps is the canonical example. Traces, partial traces and the Born rule arise from (η,ε)(\eta,\varepsilon).

Law I posits a symmetric monoidal functor M:𝑷𝒉𝒚𝒔→𝑰𝒏𝒇𝒐M:\mathbf{Phys}\to\mathbf{Info} from a category of physical systems to a category of information-theoretic structures (sets of states, density matrices, classical channels, quantum channels). Whenever a physical category has a composition (tensor product), MM preserves it. Concretely, the prototypical instance of MM sends a quantum system with Hilbert space ℋ∈𝑭𝑯𝒊𝒍𝒃\mathcal{H}\in\mathbf{FHilb} to its space of density matrices M(ℋ)={ρ∈End(ℋ):ρ≥0,Trρ=1}M(\mathcal{H})=\{\rho\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}) : \rho\geq 0,\;\mathrm{Tr}\rho=1\}, regarded as the object of statistical states; on morphisms (unitary or completely positive maps) MM acts by push-forward. We use MM in the present paper to translate “Hilbert space of microstates” (an object of 𝑷𝒉𝒚𝒔\mathbf{Phys}) into “probability simplex of accessible macrostates” (an object of 𝑰𝒏𝒇𝒐\mathbf{Info}).

Law I formalises local observables as a sheaf 𝒪:Open(X)op→𝑨𝒍𝒈\mathcal{O}:\mathrm{Open}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Alg} assigning algebras of operators to open regions. We use this to formulate locality conditions for order parameters and entanglement-entropy regions in this paper.

Following Lawvere, Law I treats algebraic theories as monoidal categories whose product-preserving functors into a base category are models. We instantiate this in 3 by viewing a Landau theory as a functor BG→𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathrm{B}G\to\mathbf{Ham}.

When we cite “(L1.k)” below we are using the Law I primitive of the same number.

Outline

The paper is organised as follows. In 2 we define the category 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} of gapped Hamiltonians on a fixed lattice and introduce the equivalence relation of gap-preserving adiabatic deformation; phases are equivalence classes (i.e. connected components of 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}). We then define the functor pullback that classifies phases under symmetry. In 3 we recover Landau’s order-parameter paradigm in this language. Mathematical Framework: Phases as Equivalence Classes under Functor Pullback

The category of gapped Hamiltonians

Fix a (regular, finite or countable) lattice Λ\Lambda and a finite-dimensional on-site Hilbert space 𝔥\mathfrak{h}. Following Law I (L1.2), the total Hilbert space is the infinite tensor product (or a thermodynamic limit thereof) ℋΛ=⨂x∈Λ𝔥\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathfrak{h}, an object of (a suitable completion of) 𝑭𝑯𝒊𝒍𝒃\mathbf{FHilb}.

Definition 1 (Local Hamiltonian). A local Hamiltonian on Λ\Lambda is a sum H=∑X⊂ΛhX,hX∈End(ℋX),H \;=\; \sum_{X\subset\Lambda} h_{X}, \qquad h_{X}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{X}), where each hXh_{X} is supported on a finite region X⊂ΛX\subset\Lambda with diameter bounded by a fixed range rr, and the operator norm of hXh_{X} is uniformly bounded.

Definition 2 (Gap). A local Hamiltonian HH is gapped with gap Δ>0\Delta>0 if its spectrum on ℋΛ\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda} has a single (possibly degenerate) ground subspace separated from the rest of the spectrum by at least Δ\Delta, uniformly as |Λ|→∞|\Lambda|\to\infty.

Definition 3 (Category 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}). The category 𝑯𝒂𝒎=𝑯𝒂𝒎(Λ,𝔥)\mathbf{Ham}=\mathbf{Ham}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{h}) has:

  • objects: gapped local Hamiltonians on Λ\Lambda;

  • morphisms H0→H1H_{0}\to H_{1}: gap-preserving piecewise-smooth paths {Hs}s∈[0,1]\{H_{s}\}_{s\in[0,1]} of local Hamiltonians with Hs=0=H0H_{s=0}=H_{0}, Hs=1=H1H_{s=1}=H_{1}, and inf⁡sΔ(Hs)>0\inf_{s}\Delta(H_{s})>0;

  • composition: concatenation of paths;

  • identity: the constant path.

By construction 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} is a groupoid (every path is reversible), and morphisms are homotopic iff they are connected through a homotopy of gap-preserving paths. We will write π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}) for the set of connected components.

Remark 4. 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} is symmetric monoidal: the tensor product H⊠H′H \boxtimes H' of two Hamiltonians on disjoint lattices is again a gapped local Hamiltonian on the union lattice; gap-preserving paths tensor componentwise. This is the direct image under the matter–information functor MM (L1.3) of the symmetric monoidal structure on 𝑷𝒉𝒚𝒔\mathbf{Phys}.

Phases as functorial equivalence classes

Definition 5 (Phase). A phase of matter (in the present setting) is a connected component of 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}: 𝑷𝒉𝒂𝒔𝒆=π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎).\mathbf{Phase}\;=\; \pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}). Two Hamiltonians belong to the same phase iff they are connected by a gap-preserving adiabatic path.

Remark 6. Three points deserve emphasis. (i) 𝑷𝒉𝒂𝒔𝒆\mathbf{Phase} is a set, not a category — the phase classification forgets the choice of path. (ii) The thermodynamic limit |Λ|→∞|\Lambda|\to\infty is essential: at finite volume every two Hamiltonians can be connected by some path, but the gap typically closes uniformly only on a measure-zero subset. (iii) The lattice Λ\Lambda and on-site Hilbert space 𝔥\mathfrak{h} are part of the data; phases are defined relative to a microscopic site structure.

Definition 7 (Symmetry action). Let GG be a group acting on 𝔥\mathfrak{h} by a unitary representation ρ:G→U(𝔥)\rho:G\to U(\mathfrak{h}). The induced on-site action on ℋΛ\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda} is Ug=⨂x∈Λρ(g)U_{g}=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\rho(g). We say H∈𝑯𝒂𝒎H\in\mathbf{Ham} is GG-symmetric if [Ug,H]=0[U_{g},H]=0 for all g∈Gg\in G.

Definition 8 (Category 𝑯𝒂𝒎G\mathbf{Ham}_{G}). The category 𝑯𝒂𝒎G\mathbf{Ham}_{G} has GG-symmetric Hamiltonians as objects, and gap-preserving paths {Hs}\{H_{s}\} through GG-symmetric Hamiltonians as morphisms.

The forgetful functor U:𝑯𝒂𝒎G→𝑯𝒂𝒎U:\mathbf{Ham}_{G}\to\mathbf{Ham} remembers only the underlying Hamiltonian without its GG-equivariance. The induced map on connected components U*:π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎G)→π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)U_{*}:\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}_{G})\to\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}) is in general neither injective nor surjective: phases that are distinct under GG-symmetry can become equivalent when the symmetry is forgotten (this is precisely the SPT phenomenon, 4).

The classifying space of GG as a one-object category

For a group GG, write BG\mathrm{B}G for the one-object groupoid with morphism set GG and composition given by group multiplication. A functor BG→𝒟\mathrm{B}G\to\mathcal{D} for any category 𝒟\mathcal{D} picks out an object DD and a homomorphism G→Aut𝒟(D)G\to\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{D}}(D).

Phases under symmetry as functor pullbacks

Definition 12 (Symmetric phase). A GG-symmetric phase is an element of π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎G)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}_{G}).

The map U*:π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎G)→π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)U_{*}:\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}_{G})\to\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}) has a kernel and cokernel that encode, respectively, SPT phases (kernel) and spontaneous symmetry breaking (cokernel). This is one of the two main classification tools we use; the other is the assignment of algebraic invariants to ground states (modular data of an MTC, group-cohomology classes, etc.) which we develop in subsequent sections.

Theorem 13 (Phase-classification exact sequence). Landau Theory in Categorical Form

Before passing to topological phases we recover the Landau paradigm in the categorical language of 2. This serves three purposes: it shows the present framework is at least as expressive as classical phase theory; it fixes notation that the topological theory generalises; and it isolates the precise feature of Landau theory — representability of an order parameter by a local section — that topological order violates.

Order parameters as local sections

Definition 15 (Order parameter representation). Let GG act on 𝔥\mathfrak{h}. An order-parameter representation is a finite-dimensional unitary representation VV of GG together with a GG-equivariant local map Φx:End(𝔥)→V\Phi_{x}:\mathrm{End}(\mathfrak{h})\to V for each site x∈Λx\in\Lambda, all related by translation.

In a state ω\omega on ℋΛ\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}, the order parameter is ⟨Φx⟩ω=ω(Φx)∈V\langle\Phi_{x}\rangle_{\omega}=\omega(\Phi_{x})\in V.

Definition 16 (Landau functor). The Landau functor of a GG-symmetric Hamiltonian HH is ℒH:BG→𝑽𝒆𝒄𝒕,ℒH(*)=V,ℒH(g)=ρV(g),\mathcal{L}_{H}:\mathrm{B}G\to\mathbf{Vect}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{H}(\ast) = V, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{H}(g) = \rho_{V}(g), where VV is the order-parameter representation and the assigned map records the action of GG on the expectation ⟨Φx⟩\langle\Phi_{x}\rangle in the ground state.

Proposition 17 (Landau classification, functorial form). Two GG-symmetric Hamiltonians H0,H1H_{0},H_{1} are in the same Landau phase iff their Landau functors ℒH0\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}} and ℒH1\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}} are naturally isomorphic as functors BG→𝑽𝒆𝒄𝒕\mathrm{B}G\to\mathbf{Vect}. Equivalently, two Hamiltonians are in the same Landau phase iff their order-parameter expectations lie on the same GG-orbit (up to VV-isomorphism).

Proof. A natural isomorphism η:ℒH0⇒ℒH1\eta:\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}} is a GG-intertwiner between the order-parameter representations. Existence of such an intertwiner is exactly the condition that the order parameters lie on the same GG-orbit, which by Goldstone’s theorem and standard Landau analysis is equivalent to lying in the same gap-preserving phase . ◻

Remark 18. The Ginzburg–Landau free energy f[φ]=a(T)|φ|2+b|φ|4+c|∇φ|2+⋯f[\varphi]=a(T)|\varphi|^{2}+b|\varphi|^{4}+c|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\cdots is recovered as the action of ℒH\mathcal{L}_{H} on a generating loop in BG\mathrm{B}G, weighted by the relevant invariant polynomials in VV.

Landau is not enough

The Landau classification is precisely the data (V,⟨Φ⟩ up to orbit)(V,\langle\Phi\rangle\text{ up to orbit}). Two limitations are immediate.

  1. It is local: it sees only the value of an on-site (or short-range) functional.

  2. It is symmetry-breaking-centric: it distinguishes phases via the residual subgroup H⊆GH\subseteq G stabilising ⟨Φ⟩\langle\Phi\rangle.

A topological phase is, by definition, one where ⟨Φ⟩≡0\langle\Phi\rangle\equiv 0 for every local Φ\Phi and yet the ground subspace has structure (degeneracy, anyonic excitations, non-trivial entanglement) detectable only nonlocally. Renormalization Group as a Functor on 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}

We pause to make one further connection to Law I that will be needed by Laws III and IV: the renormalization group (RG) acting on 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}.

Block-spin RG as a coarsening functor

Fix a block-size b≥2b\geq 2 and group sites of Λ\Lambda into blocks of bdb^{d} sites in spatial dimension dd. Each block carries a Hilbert space 𝔥⊗bd\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes b^{d}} of which we project onto a chosen subspace 𝔥′↪𝔥⊗bd\mathfrak{h}'\hookrightarrow \mathfrak{h}^{\otimes b^{d}}. The induced map on Hamiltonians is the action of the block-spin RG transformation Rb:𝑯𝒂𝒎(Λ,𝔥)→𝑯𝒂𝒎(Λ′,𝔥′)R_{b}:\mathbf{Ham}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{h})\to\mathbf{Ham}(\Lambda', \mathfrak{h}') where Λ′\Lambda' is the rescaled lattice.

Proposition 36 (RG functoriality). For each b≥2b\geq 2, RbR_{b} is a functor 𝑯𝒂𝒎→𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}\to\mathbf{Ham}. Its action on morphisms is defined by intertwining gap-preserving paths through the block-projector.

Proof. We must check that gap-preserving paths {Hs}\{H_{s}\} map to gap-preserving paths {RbHs}\{R_{b}H_{s}\}. The block projector commutes with HsH_{s} at each ss in the gapped fixed-point regime, so the gap of RbHsR_{b}H_{s} is bounded below by the gap of HsH_{s} times the projector overlap, which is bounded uniformly along the path . Fixed points and phase representatives

Definition 37 (RG fixed point). H∈𝑯𝒂𝒎H\in\mathbf{Ham} is an RG fixed point (at scale bb) if RbH=HR_{b}H = H up to a gap-preserving deformation, i.e. [RbH]=[H][R_{b}H]=[H] in π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}).

Example 38. The toric code Hamiltonian [eq:toric-ham] is an exact RG fixed point: the block projector for b=2b=2 on the toric code reproduces a toric code on the coarsened lattice.

Remark 40. The set of RG fixed points up to gap-preserving deformation is a complete (and minimal) set of phase representatives: every phase contains at least one RG fixed point, and two fixed points in the same phase are related by a deformation. This justifies the practice of taking the toric code as the canonical representative of ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} topological order.

Critical points and CFT

A non-fixed but RG-invariant point — a fixed point of the RG flow on the space of Hamiltonian densities — is a critical point. By the operator-product-expansion formalism , critical points are described by conformal field theories (CFTs); the data of a CFT (central charge, primary spectrum, OPE coefficients) is the algebraic content of the critical point. We will not develop CFT here, but we note that critical points are not objects of 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} (they are gapless), so they sit in the boundary ∂𝑯𝒂𝒎\partial\mathbf{Ham} of the gapped category. Phase boundaries are exactly such critical points.

Why the trivial-action ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} has no SPT in 1D.

The vanishing H2(ℤ2,U(1))=0H^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1))=0 does not mean “no on-site ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} SPT exists”; it means that with on-site single-copy ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} symmetry alone there is no 1D SPT. The well-known cluster-state SPT in 1D is protected by ℤ2×ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}, with non-trivial class in H2(ℤ2×ℤ2,U(1))=ℤ2H^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1))=\mathbb{Z}_{2} (one of the Künneth cross-terms). This illustrates the dependence of the SPT classification on the choice of symmetry group, even at fixed dimension.

Example 5: ℤ2×ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} in 2D

In 2D, the relevant group is H3(ℤ2×ℤ2,U(1))H^{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1)). By Künneth and the standard computation, H3(ℤ2×ℤ2,U(1))≅ℤ23,H^{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1)) \cong \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{3}, giving eight distinct ℤ2×ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} SPT phases in 2D. Three of these come from the individual ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} factors (ℤ22\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2} from each copy of H3(ℤ2,U(1))=ℤ2H^{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{2},U(1))=\mathbb{Z}_{2}), and the remaining factor of ℤ2\mathbb{Z}_{2} arises from the Künneth cross-term, corresponding to a mixed mutual-statistics SPT.

Example 6: Toric code as a stabiliser code

We expand the link between Law II and quantum information. The toric code Hamiltonian [eq:toric-ham] is the parent Hamiltonian of the stabiliser group 𝒮=⟨Av,Bp:v∈V(Λ),p∈F(Λ)⟩⊂𝒫n\mathcal{S} = \langle A_{v}, B_{p} : v\in V(\Lambda),\ p\in F(\Lambda)\rangle \subset\mathcal{P}_{n} where 𝒫n\mathcal{P}_{n} is the Pauli group on n=2L2n=2L^{2} qubits (one per edge of an L×LL\times L torus). The stabiliser group satisfies |𝒮|=2n−2|\mathcal{S}|=2^{n-2} (two relations ∏vAv=∏pBp=𝟙\prod_{v}A_{v}=\prod_{p}B_{p}=\mathbb{1}), so the codespace has dimension 2n/2n−2=42^{n}/2^{n-2}=4, matching the four-fold degeneracy on the torus.

Example 7: Stacking and the abelian-group structure on SPT phases

SPT phases form an abelian group under stacking. Given two GG-symmetric Hamiltonians H1,H2H_{1},H_{2} on disjoint copies of the lattice, H1⊠H2H_{1}\boxtimes H_{2} is a GG-symmetric Hamiltonian on the union, with the diagonal GG-action. The trivial phase is the unit, and the inverse of a layer is its time reversal.

By 21, the stacking map matches cohomology addition: [ωH1⊠H2]=[ωH1]+[ωH2],[\omega_{H_{1}\boxtimes H_{2}}] \;=\; [\omega_{H_{1}}] + [\omega_{H_{2}}], both classes lying in Hd+1(G,U(1))H^{d+1}(G,U(1)). This is the categorical statement that “stacking SPTs adds their cocycles.”

Composition Hooks for Law III

We close by stating the precise data Law III is to consume in lifting Law II to the periodically driven setting. The reader of Law III should treat this section as an interface specification.

Hook 1: The category 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} extends to 𝑯𝒂𝒎T-per\mathbf{Ham}_{T\text{-per}}

Define 𝑯𝒂𝒎T-per\mathbf{Ham}_{T\text{-per}} as the category whose objects are TT-periodic paths H:ST1→𝑯𝒂𝒎H:S^{1}_{T}\to\mathbf{Ham} landing on gapped Hamiltonians and whose morphisms are gap-preserving homotopies through TT-periodic families. The static embedding ι:𝑯𝒂𝒎↪𝑯𝒂𝒎T-per\iota:\mathbf{Ham}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Ham}_{T\text{-per}} sends HH to the constant path.

Hook 2: Symmetry group enlarges by ℤT\mathbb{Z}_{T}

The on-site symmetry GG in Law II is enlarged in Law III by the discrete time-translation symmetry ℤT\mathbb{Z}_{T} generated by the period operator. The relevant classifying space becomes BG×BℤT\mathrm{B}G\times B\mathbb{Z}_{T}, and the SPT-style classification is by Hd+2(G×ℤT,U(1))H^{d+2}(G\times\mathbb{Z}_{T},U(1)) in spatial dimension dd (the extra degree arises from the S1S^{1} factor in spacetime).

Results

We summarise the principal results of this paper.

  1. Phases as connected components. Phases of matter on a fixed lattice are elements of π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}), the connected components of the groupoid of gapped local Hamiltonians under gap-preserving adiabatic equivalence (5).

  2. Symmetric phases as functor-pullback classes. The category of GG-symmetric Hamiltonians is equivalent to the functor category [BG,𝑯𝒂𝒎0]eq[\mathrm{B}G,\mathbf{Ham}_{0}]^{\mathrm{eq}}, so symmetric phases are functorial equivalence classes (9).

  3. Landau as functorial semantics. The Landau classification is the natural-isomorphism classification of order-parameter functors ℒH:BG→𝑽𝒆𝒄𝒕\mathcal{L}_{H}: \mathrm{B}G\to\mathbf{Vect} (17).

  4. SPT as group cohomology, functorially. The classification map π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎G)→Hd+1(G,U(1))\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}_{G})\;\to\; H^{d+1}(G,U(1)) refines to a functor SPTd:𝑮𝒓𝒑→𝑨𝒃\mathrm{SPT}^{d}:\mathbf{Grp}\to\mathbf{Ab} (21).

  5. Topological order is MTC data. A (2+1)(2{+}1)D topologically ordered phase is classified by a unitary modular tensor category, arising as the Drinfeld center of a fusion category (Levin–Wen), with the data lifted from Law I via the functor 𝒵\mathcal{Z} ([eq:lift1to2]).

  6. TEE as a phase invariant. Topological entanglement entropy γ=log⁡𝒟\gamma=\log\mathcal{D} is constant on each phase (34) and is computed by the Kitaev–Preskill formula (33).

  7. Composition hooks. We have stated five precise hooks (9) by which Law III may consume Law II to produce Floquet classifications.

Discussion

Why functorial classification?

The choice to classify phases as functorial equivalence classes is not aesthetic. It is forced by the modular composition principle of the series: each law must produce data that the next law can consume as a category, not merely as a set or group. The set π0(𝑯𝒂𝒎)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{Ham}) alone is too coarse to support the lifting to Floquet phases (Law III) or to information geometry (Law IV); we need at minimum the category 𝑯𝒂𝒎G\mathbf{Ham}_{G} and the fusion/braiding data of MTCs to be available as categorical structure that the next law’s functor can act on.

What this paper does not claim

This paper does not claim:

  1. A complete classification of (3+1)(3{+}1)D topological phases (this is open; cf. open problem 3 in 12).

  2. A categorical framework for fracton order (this is genuinely open; the string-net construction does not directly apply because of the sub-extensive ground-state degeneracy).

  3. A non-perturbative bridge to quantum gravity (this is the territory of Law IV).

We are explicit that the modular framework is not a unified theory and that the classification at the topological-order level is the most rigorous tier; the lifting to Law III and beyond carries increasing speculative content.

Relation to other categorical frameworks

Our setup is closest to that of for (2+1)(2{+}1)D and to for SPT phases. The cobordism-classification programme of extends the SPT classification to fermionic and anti-unitary symmetries; the bosonic group-cohomology classification we use here is the simpler sub-case. Our explicit functorial reformulation in terms of the Law I primitives M:𝑷𝒉𝒚𝒔→𝑰𝒏𝒇𝒐M:\mathbf{Phys}\to\mathbf{Info} and the sheaf semantics is the new contribution; the underlying mathematical results are due to the cited authors.

Open Problems

We list, in order of decreasing rigor of currently available techniques, open problems that bear directly on the modular composition.

  1. Fermionic SPT classification beyond bosonic shadow. Extend 21 to a functor on the category of finite groups equipped with a ℤ2f\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}-extension, taking values in spin-cobordism cohomology ΩSpin*(BG,U(1))\Omega^{*}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{B}G,U(1)).

  2. (3+1)(3{+}1)D topological order. Is every (3+1)(3{+}1)D topological phase the boundary of a (4+1)(4{+}1)D Walker–Wang model with input fusion 2-category? A categorical answer would generalise 29 from (2+1)(2{+}1)D to (3+1)(3{+}1)D.

  3. Fracton order. Find a categorical framework that captures the sub-extensive ground-state degeneracy and immobile excitations of fracton phases. The string-net construction does not apply.

  4. Functorial RG. Make precise Wilson’s RG as a functor (or 2-functor) on 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}. Are RG fixed points exactly the rigid objects?

  5. Non-invertible categorical symmetries. Generalise the SPT classification of 20 from groups GG to fusion categories 𝒮\mathcal{S} acting as symmetries; the relevant cohomology is the second cohomology of 𝒮\mathcal{S} in U(1)U(1).

  6. Fibration of phases over the parameter space. Make precise the fibration Phases→Couplings\mathrm{Phases}\to\mathrm{Couplings} whose fibres are connected components of 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham}, and study its monodromy (this connects to Law IV’s information geometry).

Conclusion

We have provided a functorial classification of phases of matter, building strictly on the categorical primitives of Law I and producing in clean form the data Law III will consume. The principal conceptual point is that phases are equivalence classes of functors, not just states; the Landau paradigm and its topological generalisations both fit into this scheme, with the difference being the target category (Vect for Landau, Ham for SPT, MTC for topologically ordered phases). The topological entanglement entropy γ=log⁡𝒟\gamma=\log\mathcal{D} provides the principal numerical bridge between the algebraic data (the MTC) and the physical state (the ground subspace).

The composition with Law III is by replacing the symmetry group GG with G×ℤTG\times \mathbb{Z}_{T} and the static category 𝑯𝒂𝒎\mathbf{Ham} with 𝑯𝒂𝒎T-per\mathbf{Ham}_{T\text{-per}}; the mathematical content of Law III consists precisely in identifying the natural transformations of the resulting functor categories that correspond to genuinely non-equilibrium phases.

We reiterate: the framework is modular, not unified. Each law is a self-standing mathematical layer, and the emergent properties at each layer arise from the explicit composition functor stated here and in the corresponding sections of Laws III and IV.
